May 2025

A default-risk model that turns transaction behavior into lending decisions.

An XGBoost scoring pipeline for cash-advance default prediction, designed to separate high-risk applicants, expose the drivers of that risk, and support a future policy layer of calibrated tiers and cutoffs.

A credit model is rarely judged by how elegant it looks in a notebook. It is judged at the edge of a decision: approve, decline, review, price differently, ask for more information, monitor the next cohort. That is where this cash-advance default model becomes interesting.

The data describes financial behavior in fragments: balances, income, spend, overdrafts, account history, advance requests, missingness, outliers, repeat-customer assumptions, and the timing signals that were not fully available yet. The project starts with those fragments and asks a practical question: can they be turned into a risk ranking that is useful enough for lending operations?

The first clues were behavioral.

The exploratory analysis surfaced the kind of details a credit stakeholder would recognize immediately. Error rates in transactions suggested irregular payment behavior. Overdraft totals and frequent negative balances pointed toward liquidity stress. High-income customers requesting very small advances raised possible fraud questions. Repeat customers, expected to be safer, showed default rates nearly identical to new customers.

Those findings matter because credit risk is not only a math problem. It is a behavior problem. A borrower can look safe on one aggregate and risky on another. A current balance can matter differently depending on income volatility. An overdraft count can mean something different for a long-tenured account than for a new one. The model needed features that could carry those differences without pretending the data was cleaner than it was.

Outliers were kept because tree-based models can handle them better than many linear approaches, and because extreme behavior is often exactly what a risk model should notice. Missing values were treated pragmatically, with imputation and missingness indicators where appropriate. The positive class rate was around 22%, higher than many production credit-default settings, which shaped the interpretation of imbalance and class-weighting decisions.

Feature engineering turned account activity into pressure signals.

The most useful features were not demographic shortcuts. They were financial pressure signals: advance-to-income, advance-to-balance, income stability, balance volatility, low-balance frequency, net cash flow, overdrafts per 30 days, first-advance indicators, and request timing.

Some ideas had to stay on the shelf because the raw transaction-level data was not available. That constraint is important. With full transaction history, recent 30/60/90-day windows could weight current stress more heavily than old behavior. Without it, the project had to make the best use of aggregated fields and keep the next data ask clear.

The feature-selection process compared Recursive Feature Elimination with Random Forests against XGBoost feature importance. The findings were useful in a very practical way: some engineered categorical variables, including paycheck-model features, were less predictive than expected, while liquidity and behavior features carried more signal. The final feature set retained non-zero XGBoost importance features and kept the pipeline focused.

XGBoost fit the messiness of the problem.

The final model used XGBoost because the data was noisy, partially sparse, non-linear, and full of interactions that would be difficult to specify by hand. Optuna tuning improved the model without turning the exercise into a blind algorithm contest.

The tuned model reached 0.7201 AUC and 0.4401 Gini, improving roughly 0.04 AUC and 0.07 Gini over the baseline. Those numbers are useful, but the more important readout was rank ordering. Lift charts and decile analysis showed that the model could separate risk groups, with the highest-risk deciles capturing a meaningful share of defaults.

That is the difference between a model metric and a lending tool. AUC tells you the model can discriminate. Deciles begin to tell you where an operating policy could intervene.

The middle deciles are where the work continues.

The model did a reasonable job at the extremes. The harder question lived in the middle of the score distribution, where defaults and non-defaults overlap and additional features could change the decision. That is where credit analysts would likely spend time: compare borderline accounts, inspect transaction patterns, look for recent stress, and decide whether a new feature would separate risk or simply add noise.

The next data request almost writes itself. Bring in raw transaction history. Add recent-window features. Revisit class weighting under a more production-like default rate. Test calibration. Compare raw probabilities against logit-transformed scores. Create policy tiers that a business team can understand and govern.

The model was saved in a JSON-compatible form so it could be loaded across environments, but deployment would require more than loading a file. A production version would need preprocessing parity, an API or decision-engine integration, monitoring, score stability checks, retraining logic, and documentation around how cutoffs are chosen.

A probability is not a policy.

The most useful version of this project is the one that keeps going after prediction. A raw probability can rank customers, but a lending business needs calibrated scores, tiers, thresholds, exception handling, and governance. The question becomes: what should happen when a customer lands in each band?

That final step is where model development meets leadership. The modeler has to explain the tradeoff between approval rate and loss exposure. The credit team has to decide which risks are acceptable. Operations needs a workflow for review. Monitoring needs to catch drift. The customer experience has to remain fair and understandable.

The notebook proves there is signal. The case study should make the decision path visible: account behavior becomes features, features become a score, the score becomes a risk tier, and the risk tier becomes an action someone can defend.
